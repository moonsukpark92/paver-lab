## What you see

A permeable paver sidewalk passes acceptance testing. Two years later it ponds in the rain.
Pull the test certificate and nothing is wrong — the coefficient of permeability cleared the limit.

The certificate is not lying. It simply never measured the paver's second year.

## Why

Permeability is measured on clean specimens straight from the plant. Pore space is empty, so water passes.

Pores in a real street are not empty. Soil, tyre wear particles, crushed leaves and de-icing residue settle in.
That is clogging. What a permeable paver loses is not strength but **connected path**. Break the connected
porosity and the coefficient drops even though total porosity is unchanged.

Seoul's regulation moved the axis of the test accordingly. Its permeability *durability* verification
**contaminates the specimen with fines first, then measures permeability** — grading performance after
fouling rather than when new. As a rule, only grade 3 or better may be used; ungraded products are excluded. [2]

The most important line is not in the table:

> The acceptance limit is 0.1 mm/s, but holding that 0.1 over service life reportedly requires
> **≥ 1.0 mm/s at acceptance**. [2]

Ten times the limit, to meet the limit. If true, any mix designed to hit 0.1 at acceptance has
already failed on the drawing board. We have not yet confirmed this against a primary source. <span class="badge badge--unverified">unverified</span>

## So what

- **Owners** — check whether the certificate carries a durability grade, not just a permeability number.
  KS requires four items for permeable units (appearance/dimensions, flexural strength, permeability,
  face layer thickness); certificates arriving on site with only two of them have been reported. [1][3]
- **Manufacturers** — design the mix against the post-fouling value, not the acceptance value.
- **Designers** — put the cleaning interval in the specification. A permeable pavement laid without a
  maintenance budget is an expensive impermeable pavement.

## Open questions

1. How closely does the contamination procedure resemble real street fines?
2. What are the quantitative boundaries between grades 1, 2 and 3?
3. Where does the "1.0 mm/s at acceptance" figure originate, and on what decay curve?
4. How much permeability does twice-yearly cleaning actually recover? Without that number the interval has no basis.
